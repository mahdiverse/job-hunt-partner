# Portfolio and work sample audits

A portfolio is whatever proves the user can do the work. Its form depends on the field:

| Field | Usual proof |
|---|---|
| Design (product, UX, visual, brand, motion) | Website with case studies, Dribbble or Behance, a motion reel |
| Software and data | GitHub, deployed projects, notebooks, technical write-ups, dashboards |
| Writing, content, comms | Published clips, a writing samples page, a newsletter |
| Marketing and growth | Campaign case studies with results, a one-page results summary |
| Product management | Product write-ups, launch docs (with confidential details removed), talks |
| Research and academia | Publications, Google Scholar, talks, posters |
| Trades, healthcare, education | Certifications, licences, lesson plans, photos of finished work, references |

If the user's field is not in the table, ask: "What do people hiring in your field look at to judge your work?"

## How reviewers actually read

Reviewers skim first, and only read closely if the skim earns it. Estimates of the first pass for design portfolios range from about 90 seconds to three minutes, with most of that time spent on the first case study and the about page. Treat those numbers as rough, but the lesson holds in every field: the first screen and the first piece decide whether anything else gets seen.

Depth beats volume. Two to four deep pieces that show judgment do more than ten thumbnails.

## Audit in this order

Check the first screen, then the first piece, then everything else.

### 1. The first screen

- In one sentence: who is this, what do they do, and for whom? If you cannot answer that, it is the top finding.
- Is the most relevant work for their target roles first?
- Clear contact route and a resume link.
- Loads fast and works on a phone. Many reviewers open links from email on mobile.

### 2. Each piece of work

- **Context.** What the problem was and why it mattered, in a few lines.
- **Their role.** What they personally did. Most work is team work, so separating "I" from "we" is what reviewers look for. Flag any piece where you cannot tell.
- **Constraints.** Time, budget, technical limits, compliance. These make the decisions judgeable.
- **Decisions and trade-offs.** The options considered and why one won. This is the main evidence of seniority.
- **Outcome.** A real result, with a number if one exists. If nothing was measured, a strong piece says so plainly and says what it would measure next time. Honest gaps read better than vague claims.
- **Artifacts.** Screens, code, samples or photos that prove the work exists, not just describe it.
- **Scannable.** Headings, short paragraphs, visuals doing work. A dense wall of text gets skipped.

### 3. Fit to target roles

- Does the selection match the roles in the profile? A portfolio aimed at the job they had, not the one they want, is a common miss.
- Is anything missing that the target roles will expect to see?
- If AI tools are part of how they work, does a piece show how (what tool, what it produced, what the person changed)? Vague claims about an "AI-augmented workflow" read as filler. Specific ones read as skill.

### 4. Hygiene

- Broken links, dead demos, empty pages, "coming soon".
- Confidential work: is it handled with permission, redaction or a password, and is the reason stated?
- Outdated work leading the page.
- Inconsistency with the resume or LinkedIn in titles, dates and project names.

## Output

1. One line on overall state: what it does well and the main problem.
2. All findings, ranked High, Medium or Low impact, each with a concrete fix.
3. Quick wins, meaning fixes under an hour, called out separately.
4. If they asked, a suggested order for the work and a rewrite of the opening line.

Do not invent outcomes for their case studies. Where a piece needs a result, ask them for it.
