# Resumes, CVs and cover letters

## Pick the mode first

Ask which one the user wants if it is not obvious, because they produce very different work:

1. **Audit.** Review the resume as it stands and rank every issue.
2. **Adapt for a role.** Reorder and select from the master resume for one posting. Change as little as possible.
3. **Rewrite.** Rework bullets and structure. Only when the audit shows the base resume is weak, or the user asks.
4. **Build from scratch.** Interview them for the raw material first: roles, dates, what they did, what changed because of it.
5. **Cover letter.** Only when the posting asks for one, or the user wants one.

If the user says their resume is already strong, believe them and default to mode 2. Polishing a good resume into generic copy is a common way to make it worse.

When the user gives a file (.docx, Google Doc, PDF), return the edits in the same format where possible, and keep their layout unless the layout is the problem.

## Country norms

Ask which country they are applying in. Norms differ:

- **US and Canada:** "resume", one page for early career, two for experienced. No photo, date of birth or marital status.
- **UK and Ireland:** "CV", usually two pages. No photo.
- **Much of continental Europe:** photos and personal details are more common in some countries, such as Germany. Follow what the user's local market expects, and when unsure, leave personal details out.
- **Academic and some public sector roles:** a long-form CV with publications is normal.

When in doubt, follow the norm of the country where the employer is hiring, not where the user lives.

## What gets a resume through

Two readers see it: software first, then a person who scans fast.

**For the software (applicant tracking systems and AI screeners):**

- Single column, standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills), no text inside images, tables or text boxes.
- A text-based PDF or a .docx. Both parse reliably in most modern systems. Test a PDF by selecting its text. If the text will not select, it will not parse.
- Use the plain industry name for skills and tools, the way postings in their field write them. Write "Google Analytics", not "web analytics suite".
- Newer screeners judge skills in context, so a skill named inside a bullet that shows it in use counts for more than a skills list. Repeating keywords does not help.

**For the person:**

- The top third does the work. Name, target title, a short summary and the most relevant role should tell the reader who this is in seconds.
- Bullets follow **action, scope, result**: what they did, how big it was, what changed. Harvard's career office template puts it as: begin each line with an action verb, and quantify where possible.
- Three to five bullets per recent role, fewer for older ones. Drop roles from long ago that add nothing.
- Cut empty traits such as "results-oriented", "team player" and "hard worker". Show the trait with a fact instead.
- Dates, titles and employers match LinkedIn. Recruiters check.

## Numbers: real or none

Quantify only from what the user can back up. If there is no number, use a concrete outcome instead: what shipped, who used it, what stopped breaking. Never invent a percentage to make a bullet look finished. Ask the user: "Do you know roughly how many, how much, or how often?"

Keep causal hedges exactly as the user wrote them. "Joined during a period when sign-ups doubled" is a different claim from "doubled sign-ups".

## Adapting for one role

The master resume is the source of truth. Adapting means selecting and reordering it, not inventing new content.

1. **Read the title, level and team first,** then the job description. The title says what someone in the role does all day. Job description text is often boilerplate.
2. **Reorder** the summary, bullets and skills so the most relevant proof comes first.
3. **Select.** Drop bullets that do not help this application. A shorter, sharper resume is fine.
4. **Mirror terms only for things they really have.** If the posting says "stakeholder management" and the user has done it under another name, use the posting's term. If they have not done it, leave it out. Adding a skill they cannot defend is fabrication, and it surfaces in the interview.
5. **List what moved and why** in one line at the end, so the user can check it.
6. **Do not claim:** list anything a reader might infer that the user cannot defend.

## Audit checklist

Rank every finding as High, Medium or Low impact.

- Does the top third say who they are and what they want, in seconds?
- Would the summary read the same for three other people? If so, it is generic.
- Is each bullet a result, or a job duty?
- Any claim the user could not defend in an interview?
- Any number without a source?
- Parsing risks: columns, tables, icons, text in images, headers and footers carrying key information?
- Length right for their level and country?
- Consistent with LinkedIn: titles, dates, employers?
- Does it sound like one specific person, or like generic AI output? In TopResume's 2025 survey of 600 US hiring managers, about one in five said they would reject a resume or cover letter that looked fully AI-generated.
- Does it read as the level they are targeting? Watch for senior people describing production tasks, and junior people inflating scope.
- File name: "Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf", not "resume_final_v3.pdf".

Output: one line on the overall state, the ranked findings, then rewritten lines only for the ones that need it. Do not rewrite the whole document unless asked.

## Cover letters

Write one only when asked or required. Keep it under about 250 words. Three moves:

1. Why this role at this company, through one specific detail about their product, team or problem, not their mission statement.
2. One or two proof points from the resume that fit that detail, told as a short story with a result.
3. A plain close. No "I am confident I would be a great fit".

The same truth rules apply. Use no claims that are not in the user's materials.
