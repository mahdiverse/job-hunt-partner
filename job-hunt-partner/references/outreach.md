# Outreach

Read this before writing any message, then read outreach-examples.md to calibrate tone and length.

## Why outreach matters

Referrals convert far better than cold applications. In Lever's hiring data (reported by SHRM, 2016), about 1 in 16 referred candidates was hired, against 1 in 152 who applied through a careers page or job posting. The numbers vary by company and are old, but the gap is large and consistent across later studies.

## The numbers behind the sequence

Most public LinkedIn data comes from sales outreach, not job seekers, so treat it as the shape of the problem rather than a forecast. Across 13.2 million outreach attempts from May 2025 to April 2026, Expandi measured:

- About 28.5% of connection requests accepted.
- About 3% of connection notes replied to, falling from 3.5% to 2.2% over the year.
- About 10.4% of messages sent after an accept got a reply, steady all year.

The lesson: **the note earns the accept, and the conversation happens in the message after.** Writing the note as a conversation opener plans around the 3%. Write it for the accept.

## LinkedIn limits

Check these against what the user's composer shows. LinkedIn changes them, and the composer is the final word.

| Surface | Limit |
|---|---|
| Connection note | 200 characters per LinkedIn Help (as reported September 2026). Some Premium accounts still show 300. Write to 200 unless the user confirms their composer allows more. |
| Notes on a free account | Only a few per month. LinkedIn Help says three, and some users report five. Premium has no monthly cap. |
| DM to a first-degree connection | Long limit, but keep it under 400 characters. |
| InMail | Premium only, limited credits. |

**Free account strategy.** Send connection requests without a note to most people and save the few notes for the contacts who matter most. In Belkins' 2026 study of 15 million touchpoints, requests without a note were accepted slightly more often (27.6% against 25.3%), but people who accepted a noted request replied more often later (8.2% against 5.3%). So a note helps where you want a conversation, not where you only want an accept.

## The sequence

Four moves at most. Postings close fast, so do not run a slow build on a live role.

**Move 1. Connection note.** Day 0. No question, no ask. Role or reason, one hook, one proof, plain close. State the character count.

**Move 2. The DM.** 24 to 48 hours after they accept. Under 400 characters. The ask goes here, named in plain words. One ask per message.

**Move 3. The packet.** Within the hour of a yes. Everything they need to act in under a minute (template below).

**Move 4. One follow-up.** 7 to 10 days after silence. Two sentences, no new ask. Then stop. That contact is closed for that role.

Before drafting a cold message, ask whether the user already has a live thread with that person on any channel. A warm thread wins, and the cold message is cancelled.

**Contacts per role:** two at most. One peer or hiring manager before applying, and the recruiter after applying.

## Name the ask by move 2

By move 2 the recipient should know what kind of message this is. If it is about an application, say so. If it is a conversation, keep it one, and let any ask come later or not at all.

The failure this prevents: move 1 opens an interesting problem, move 2 asks for help. People engage on one premise, receive another, and stop reading.

## The ask ladder

Pick the rung the relationship supports. One rung too high is worse than one too low, because a referral puts the referrer's reputation at stake. The ask has to be easy to decline.

**Rung 0. No relationship.** Ask for routing.
> "Do you know who's leading hiring for this one?"

**Rung 1. Thin relationship.** Same school, a mutual connection, or one exchange.
> "Would you be comfortable putting in a referral? Completely fine if not."

**Rung 2. Real exchange.** A call happened, or a genuine back and forth.
> The same ask, with the packet ready and "happy to answer anything before you decide."

**Rung 3. They offered.** Take it. Send the packet within the hour.

**Already applied, no referral basis:**
> "I applied on Monday. If you think it's worth a look, I'd appreciate you flagging it to the team. Totally fine if not."

Always include the exit line. It is what makes a yes mean something.

## Referral timing

Ask before applying wherever a referral is plausible. Many applicant tracking systems only credit a referral that comes before the application. This varies by employer, so do not claim how a specific company handles it.

If the user already applied, ask the process question instead:
> "I already applied. Does your referral system still let you add one, or is it too late?"

Never suggest withdrawing and reapplying.

## Conversation invites

Use these when there is no live posting, the company is interesting but not hiring, the person's work is worth learning from, they are alumni, or after a rejection worth keeping warm.

**Register.** Match it to the user's level. Experienced people trade as peers, so the message offers something or asks a precise question. Early-career people can ask for advice openly, but still ask one specific question, not "any advice?".

**Structure:**

1. Who the user is, in one line.
2. Why this person: one thing they built, shipped, wrote, said or decided.
3. One narrow question they can answer without preparing.
4. Two options: 15 to 20 minutes, or just answer in a message. The message option costs them nothing and makes a yes more likely.

**The user proposes times:** two or three windows, in both time zones. Do not send a scheduling link to someone doing the user a favour.

**After the conversation:** within 24 hours, send something useful instead of a plain thank you. If they named a problem, send a link, a short write-up or a sketch that helps. Referral offers often come at this point unprompted.

**What the user can trade.** Ask them: "What do you know or do that people in your field are still figuring out?" Leading with that beats leading with a request.

## Building each message

Default structure: **context, specific hook, proof, close.** A connection note usually needs three of the four.

**Context.** State the relevant action fast. "I just applied for the Data Analyst role." Do not spend the opening on praise.

**Hook.** One real detail turned into a reason. Take it from the product, the team, the title or the person's own work before the job description, since posting text is generic and a hook built on it reads generic. Paraphrase. Never paste a long phrase from the posting. For a conversation invite, the hook is about the person, not the company.

**Proof.** One concrete proof point from the user's materials. A second only in a longer DM, and only if both earn their place. Evidence beats adjectives: cut "perfect fit", "ideal candidate" and "deeply passionate".

**Close.** The least demanding close that can work.

- Connection note: "Would be great to connect."
- DM: the ask, named, with an exit.
- Conversation invite: two options. "Worth 15 minutes, or happy to ask it here if that's easier."
- Follow-up: "Following up in case this got buried. Either way, good luck with the search."

## Editing rules

- **One angle.** Do not compress the resume into the message. Pick the single overlap most likely to interest this reader.
- **Make relevance explicit.** Not "this line caught my attention", but why it is relevant to the user.
- **Cut defensiveness.** Delete lines that argue with imaginary competitors ("unlike most candidates...").
- **Cut weak qualifiers:** "just wanted to", "I hope this isn't too forward", "if it's worth a conversation", "either way".
- **Links.** A portfolio or GitHub link goes in a DM when it earns its space, not in a connection note, where it crowds out the reason to connect.

**Phrases to avoid:** "pick your brain", "grab coffee sometime", "I'd love to learn from you", "I was inspired by your journey", "your mission deeply resonates with me", "I know you must be busy", "I'm sure I'd be a great fit", "Is there anything worth highlighting?" (an ambiguous referral ask in disguise), and "happy to jump on a call" in a first message.

## The referral packet

Send it the moment someone agrees. It should take them under a minute.

```
Thanks, that means a lot. Everything in one place:

Role: [exact title]
Link: [direct job URL]
Req ID: [if there is one]
Applied under: [the email on the application, or "not yet applied"]

One line you can paste:
"[About 25 words from the resume, ending on a fact.]"

Resume attached. Happy to answer anything if they come back to you.
```

Never send a wall of context, and never make them ask a follow-up question to help.

## Length limits

| Message | Limit |
|---|---|
| Connection note | 200 characters unless the composer allows more. State the count. |
| DM after accept | under 400 characters |
| Conversation invite | under 600 characters |
| Follow-up on silence | two sentences |
| Comment on someone's post | two sentences, plain and factual |

## Timing

- Between their accept and the DM: 24 to 48 hours.
- After a reply or a call: follow up within 24 hours.
- After silence: one follow-up at 7 to 10 days, then stop. A follow-up after one day of silence reads as pressure.

## Quality check before returning any message

- Grounded in one specific part of the role, or one specific thing this person did?
- Exactly one main proof point, traceable to the user's materials?
- Is the ask named, and on the right rung?
- Exactly one ask, with an exit line where needed?
- No question in a connection note?
- Within the character limit, with the count stated?
- Free of the phrases above and of em dashes?
- Would a real person in the user's field send this?

## Output format

1. One or two sentences diagnosing the draft, if the user gave one: what works and the main problem.
2. The message.
3. Character count for notes and DMs.
4. Which move this is, and what comes next.

Give one version by default. Offer options only when asked, or when the right approach to this recipient is genuinely unclear. Do not explain edits line by line unless asked.

If outreach is in a language other than English, apply the same structure and adapt the politeness to local norms, which can be more formal than these defaults.
