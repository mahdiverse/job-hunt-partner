# Finding and evaluating roles

## Where to look

Ask the user which boards their field actually hires through, then add the general ones. Defaults:

- **General:** LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed, Glassdoor, the company's own careers page.
- **ATS boards:** Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Workable and SmartRecruiters host many company boards directly and are often more current than aggregators.
- **By field:** for example Wellfound and YC's Work at a Startup for startups, Dribbble and Behance for designers, Hacker News "Who is hiring" for engineers, idealist for nonprofits, government portals for public sector roles. Ask the user for the niche boards their field uses rather than guessing.
- **Hiring posts:** LinkedIn content search is noisy. Quoted searches like "hiring" plus a title mostly return job seekers' own posts. Use hirer phrasing such as "we are hiring" or "my team is hiring".

Prefer recent postings. Old postings are more likely to be filled, frozen or never real (see ghost jobs below).

Claim to have reviewed only pages you actually loaded.

## Gates: pass or fail before any scoring

Check these against the profile. A role that fails a gate gets one line in chat and nothing else.

1. **Location and work rights.** Read the company's own wording, not the job board tag. Board tags like "Remote" are often wrong or partial: "remote" can mean remote within one country, and "remote Europe" often means within the EU or EEA only. Where a hiring manager's post or the careers page is stricter than the listing, the stricter one wins.
2. **Level.** Skip roles well below or well above the user's level unless the profile says they are open to it.
3. **Pay.** If a band is published and its top is below the user's floor, fail. If pay is not disclosed, write "Not disclosed" and pass the gate. Never estimate. Keep published bands separate from third-party estimates.
4. **Dealbreakers** from the profile.

If the user needs sponsorship or has a work-rights constraint, flag what applies (sponsorship stated or not, payroll entity in their country or not, regulated industries that screen nationality). Flag it, do not silently score it down.

## Ghost jobs and scams

**Ghost jobs** are postings with no real intent to hire. Greenhouse estimated in early 2025 that 18 to 22% of jobs on its platform were ghost jobs. Signals:

- Posted for months, or reposted again and again with the same text.
- Not listed on the company's own careers page.
- Vague scope with no named team, product or manager.
- Recent news of layoffs or a hiring freeze.

A role open for months is not always a ghost job. Sometimes the company has not found the specialist it described, which makes an imperfect match worth a try. Say which reading the evidence supports.

**Scams** have grown sharply. The FTC reports job scam losses rose from about $90 million in 2020 to over $500 million in 2024. Warn the user and stop if you see:

- Any request to pay: for training, equipment, a background check, or to "unlock" earnings.
- First contact by text, WhatsApp or Telegram from a "recruiter" they never contacted.
- Interviews held only over chat, or an offer with no real interview.
- Requests for a national ID number or bank details before a written offer.
- Simple "tasks" (rating products, liking videos) that pay commission.

Tell them to verify through the company's official website and a recruiter's real company email, never through links in the message.

## Research before the verdict

For any role that passes the gates, check:

- What the company actually builds or does, and for whom.
- Recent news: funding, launches, layoffs, leadership changes.
- How the team describes its own work (engineering blog, design blog, talks, posts).
- Team size and where the role reports.
- How long the posting has been up, and whether it was reposted.
- Whether the user already applied there, or already knows someone there.

## Scoring

Read the title, level, team and product first. These say what the person in the seat actually does all day. Then use the job description for hard requirements and eligibility facts. Job descriptions are often written from a template, so treat their wish lists as soft unless a requirement is clearly central.

Score 0 to 100:

| Factor | Weight | What you are judging |
|---|---|---|
| Core work match | 35 | Can they do the main work of this role, based on things they have done? |
| Level and scope | 20 | Does the scope match their experience, not just the title? |
| Proof they can show | 15 | Can they point to evidence (resume lines, portfolio pieces, results) for the core work? |
| Domain or industry | 15 | Have they worked in this space, or something close enough to transfer? |
| Role reality | 15 | Is this a real, stable, well-run opening? Includes ghost job signals, company health, and effort compared with odds. |

Recommendation:

- **80 to 100: Strong match**
- **65 to 79: Worth applying**
- **50 to 64: Possible but low priority**
- **Below 50: Not recommended**

Never inflate to be encouraging. A user applying to the wrong roles loses weeks.

Next action, pick one: Apply now, Ask for a referral first, Fix resume first, Contact the hiring manager or recruiter, Research further, Save for later, Skip.

Referral before applying: many applicant tracking systems attach a referral only when it comes before the application, so where a referral is plausible, the next action is usually "Ask for a referral first". This varies by employer, so do not claim how a specific company works.

## Verdict format

For each role worth pursuing:

```
[Company] - [Title]
Link | Location policy (their words) | Pay: [band or Not disclosed] | Posted: [date]
Score: NN/100, [Recommendation]
What lands: one or two lines
What does not: one or two lines
Next action: [one]
Do not claim: up to five short lines on things a reader might infer that the user cannot defend
```

For roles that fail a gate or score Not recommended, one line in chat: company, title, reason. Do not log them unless the profile says to.
